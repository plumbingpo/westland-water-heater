A typical tank water heater gives eight to twelve years of service in Westland, MI, and hard water, high pressure, and skipped maintenance can shorten that. Most tanks give a few warnings near the end: water seeping from under the jacket, rust in the hot water, sediment noise that gets louder by the month, and showers that go cold sooner every winter. A leak from the tank body is the clearest signal of all. When the glass lining gives out and the steel corrodes through, there is no part to replace, and a tank that is weeping today can let go without warning tomorrow.

Picking the replacement is where most of the value is. The old heater's size is only a starting point, since families grow, bathrooms get added, and a 40-gallon tank that was fine a decade ago may now run out every morning. Tanks get sized by first-hour rating, tankless units by gallons per minute at winter temperature rise, and we explain what each option really costs to buy and to run. A like-for-like gas or electric tank is usually the simplest and least expensive swap. Tankless and heat pump models carry a higher price but can trim operating costs, and they come with their own requirements for gas line size, venting, electrical capacity, or room to breathe. We lay out the facts, then you decide.

On installation day, every step gets verified. We drain and remove the old heater, set the new one level, and install fresh supply connectors and a working shutoff. On gas units, the connection gets a sediment trap and a leak test, and the vent is built to the manufacturer's spec. The relief valve discharge goes where it should, and any expansion tank, drain pan, earthquake straps, or permit your local code requires is included. Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • Rusty or brown water from the hot side only
  • Rust or corrosion on the tank, fittings, or vent connector
  • Loud rumbling or popping that flushing no longer fixes
  • A slow leak or damp spot under the tank itself
  • Energy bills rising while hot water gets harder to come by
  • A heater older than most tanks last, usually eight to twelve years
  • Less and less hot water, even after repairs
  • Repairs that keep coming back, one part after another

Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Follow these tips from our technicians:

  • Have the anode rod checked around year three to five and replaced if it is worn
  • Tankless units need a yearly descaling, especially where the water is hard
  • Start a flushing routine in the first year, before sediment hardens
  • Set the thermostat around 120 degrees to prevent scalding and reduce wear
  • Keep household water pressure in a safe range to protect the tank and its fittings
  • Clean the air filter on a heat pump water heater every few months
  • Note the install date and keep up with maintenance so the next replacement is never an emergency
