A gas water heater is a fairly simple machine, which is part of why it lasts. A burner under the tank heats the water, exhaust rises through a flue that runs up the center of the tank, and a gas control valve on the front acts as both thermostat and safety shutoff. That valve only lets gas reach the burner while it senses a flame, through a thermocouple on standing-pilot models or a flame sensor on electronic ignition models. Most failures trace back to one of those parts. In Westland, MI, we regularly find thermocouples that no longer hold the valve open, pilots starved by a lint-clogged flame arrestor screen, gas valves that have failed internally, and flue or vent problems that let exhaust spill back into the room.

Diagnosing a gas water heater properly means checking combustion, not just parts. Across Westland, our technicians measure thermocouple output in millivolts, study the pilot and burner flame for color and shape, read gas pressure at the valve, and test draft at the hood so we know exhaust is leaving the house. We check the combustion chamber for soot and scorching, which point to a blocked flue or poor combustion air, and we test for carbon monoxide near the heater. Keep one rule in mind: a gas smell means leaving the house and calling the gas utility from outside before anything else, and Westland Water Heater can take over from there.

Once we know the cause, the repair is usually simple. A new thermocouple or flame sensor, a cleaned pilot tube and burner, a cleared flame arrestor screen, or a replacement gas control valve gets most heaters running again the same day. Vent problems, like a disconnected connector or a flue pipe without enough rise, are corrected on the spot. If sediment is rumbling in the tank, we flush it, and if the anode rod is spent, we can replace it. When replacement is the smarter move, we install new gas heaters sized to your household's first-hour demand, with proper atmospheric, power vent, or direct vent venting, a drip leg on the gas supply, and the expansion tank, drain pan, and strapping your local code calls for. How to Tell Something Is Wrong

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you money and keep a slow drip from turning into a flooded utility room. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • Pilot outages that keep coming back after a relight
  • Leaks at the drain valve, the fittings, or the bottom of the tank
  • Popping, rumbling, or crackling sounds as the burner heats
  • A cold tank and a burner that will not come on
  • A rotten-egg odor near the water heater, in which case get out and call the gas company first
  • A yellow, lazy, or sooty burner flame instead of a crisp blue one
  • Signs of backdrafting, such as soot, condensation, or an exhaust smell at the vent
  • Hot water that runs out faster than it used to

Why Professional Gas Water Heater Services Matters

Consider the risks first. Every gas water heater involves fuel piping, an open flame, and exhaust that contains carbon monoxide. A loose flare or threaded joint can leak gas, a vent that is disconnected or poorly sloped can spill exhaust back into the room, and a gas valve installed without a leak test is a risk you cannot see. Then there is the diagnostic side: a pilot that goes out can mean a weak thermocouple, a dirty pilot orifice, a clogged flame arrestor, a draft problem, or a failing gas valve, and replacing them one by one is slow and costly.

Professional service gets you a correct answer and a safe appliance. Instead of trial and error, we confirm thermocouple output, gas pressure, burner flame, and draft with proper tools. Every gas connection we make is tested for leaks before we leave, and vent connectors are secured with the right slope and clearances. When a replacement is needed, we size the new heater by first-hour rating, handle the permit where one is required, and add the expansion tank, sediment trap, drain pan, or seismic straps your home needs. Prevention Tips That Actually Help

A little routine care goes a long way toward keeping a water heater running well past its expected lifespan. Put these technician-approved steps to work at home:

  • Check the anode rod every three to five years, since it protects the tank from corrosion
  • Install carbon monoxide alarms on every level of the home and test them monthly
  • Watch for a loose, rusted, or sagging vent pipe above the heater
  • Set the gas valve near 120 degrees, because hotter water costs more and can scald
  • Never store flammable liquids near a gas water heater
  • Vacuum lint and dust from the flame arrestor screen at the base a few times a year
  • Flush a few gallons from the drain valve periodically to control sediment
