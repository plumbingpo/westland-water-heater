Two quiet processes decide how long a water heater lasts: sediment and corrosion. Sediment comes from minerals that separate out as water is heated and sink to the bottom of the tank, where they steal efficiency, cause popping and rumbling on gas models, and overheat the steel or bury the lower element on electric ones. Corrosion is held back by the anode rod, a sacrificial rod of magnesium or aluminum that wears away so the tank lining is spared. Neither one is visible from the outside, and by the time a homeowner notices, the tank is often leaking. That is the reason maintenance matters for Westland, MI homes: it interrupts both problems while they are still cheap to fix.

Each visit follows the same thorough checklist. The tank is drained and flushed to clear the sediment layer. The anode rod comes out for inspection and is replaced when little of it remains. The T&P relief valve is tested so we know it will open under pressure and close again cleanly. On gas models, we check the burner, pilot assembly, thermocouple or flame sensor, flue, and draft. Electric heaters get element and thermostat testing and a check of the wiring connections. We also measure the air charge in the expansion tank, check the water pressure, set the thermostat around 120 degrees unless a mixing valve allows higher storage, and inspect the drain valve, connections, and pan.

Tankless heaters skip the sediment bed but have a scale problem of their own. Minerals coat the heat exchanger over time, reducing efficiency and eventually causing shutdowns. Our technicians connect a circulation pump to the isolation valves and run a descaling solution through the exchanger, clean the inlet screen, inspect the venting, and check the condensate drain on condensing models. How to Tell Something Is Wrong

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • Small drips at the drain valve or connections
  • More than a year since anyone flushed the tank
  • Energy bills creeping up with no change in hot water use
  • Noises from the water heater that were not there before
  • A smell like rotten eggs that shows up only on the hot side
  • Hot water running out sooner than it used to
  • A tankless unit showing error codes or fluctuating temperatures
  • Hot water that looks cloudy or rust-colored

Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Glance at the pan and connections regularly so small leaks are caught early
  • Ask about water treatment if flushing turns up heavy mineral buildup
  • Plan yearly descaling for a tankless heater, sooner if scale warnings appear
  • Ask about the anode rod at each visit; it is the tank's main defense against rust
  • Schedule a flush every year, or every six months if you have hard water
  • Leave open space around the tank, especially near a gas burner
  • Keep the thermostat near 120 degrees to limit scalding and slow sediment buildup
